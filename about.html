About the Lexicon

The AVALANCHE Project

The AVALANCHE Lexicon is developed as part of Work Package 11 of the AVALANCHE project, funded by the European Union’s CERV Programme on European Remembrance (Grant Agreement 101143807).

The project runs from May 2024 to April 2026 and brings together partners across Europe — Mubat (coordinator, Italy), Fondazione CDEC (Italy), Aristotle University of Thessaloniki (Greece), and UVSQ (France) — to develop new approaches to Holocaust education through oral history archives.

What the lexicon publishes

Alongside the lexicon of approximately 400 headwords, the platform publishes several companion resources:

  • Gender keyword lists showing which words are distinctively more frequent in women’s or men’s testimonies — revealing differences in how female and male survivors, victims, and witnesses express their memories of the same events.
  • Subcorpus keyword lists comparing the vocabulary of different communities — for example, Jewish survivors in mainland Italy versus the Jewish community of Rhodes — to surface how geography and community shape the language of testimony.
  • Word pairs (collocations) that co-occur in each subcorpus, ranked by how strongly the two words are drawn to each other — pairs like camera + gas or fame + sete (hunger + thirst) that reveal the recurring vocabulary of testimony.
  • Cross-lingual word pairs: 88 Greek–Italian equivalents compared through their collocates and modifiers, highlighting where the two countries’ experiences converge and where they diverge.

Methodology

Note: This lexicon is an exploratory corpus linguistics tool. It surfaces distributional patterns — how often words appear, where they cluster, and what they co-occur with — as evidence for researchers, exhibition visitors, and educators to interpret. It does not make historical claims.

Corpus

The lexicon draws on 397 oral history testimonies (~2.73 million words) in two languages: 261 in Greek (~1.92M words) and 136 in Italian (~0.81M words).

The Greek corpus comprises testimonies from three archives: USHMM (53 testimonies, 13 female / 40 male, ~392K words), Memories of the Occupation in Greece — MoG (27 testimonies, all female, ~355K words), and Istorima (181 testimonies, all female, ~1.17M words).

The Italian corpus consists of CDEC testimonies from Italy (101 testimonies, 48 female / 53 male, ~645K words) and Rhodes (35 testimonies, 30 female / 5 male, ~168K words).

Italian testimonies and the USHMM Greek testimonies were transcribed using Automatic Speech Recognition (Whisper Large v3, run locally). MoG and Istorima have manual transcriptions. Minimum frequency thresholds are applied throughout the pipeline to filter out ASR artifacts.

Natural Language Processing

Testimonies were processed using spaCy NLP pipelines (el_core_news_lg for Greek, it_core_news_lg for Italian), extracting lemmas and parts of speech according to the Universal Dependencies tagset. Only content words (nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs, proper nouns) are retained for analysis; function words (determiners, pronouns, prepositions, conjunctions, particles) and numbers are excluded. Greek lemmas undergo accent normalization to merge diacritical variants.

The pipeline attempts to reduce each word to its dictionary form (e.g. andavamo → andare, ερχόταν → έρχομαι) and tag it with its grammatical role. Italian lemmatization is generally reliable, but Greek lemmatization is less accurate — some inflected forms may appear as separate entries.

Headword Selection

The lexicon contains approximately 400 headwords selected through a multi-step process. All content words meeting minimum frequency (at least 30 total occurrences) and dispersion thresholds (appearing in at least 3 testimonies) are tested for statistical distinctiveness. Words that are distinctive in at least one subcorpus comparison are retained, and a manual curation step removes leaked stopwords and discourse markers. Additionally, some words that do not pass these thresholds are manually included when they are judged to be of interest — since keyword analysis by design surfaces differences between groups, not commonalities, and some historically important words may be shared across all subcorpora.

Most headwords are words that are both frequent enough to be reliable and used notably more by one group of survivors than another. A smaller number are manually added because they carry historical weight even if shared across all groups.

Keyword Analysis

Keywords are words that are used significantly more by one group of survivors than another. On the site, words are ranked by frequency ratio (×N) — how many times more frequent a word is in one group compared to the other. For example, ×4.1 means about four times more frequent.

Only words that pass a statistical significance test are shown, ensuring the differences are likely real and not due to chance. A minimum frequency of 10 on each side and appearance in at least 3 testimonies are required to prevent single-testimony spikes.

Comparisons are conducted within the same archive to avoid confounding variables: Italian CDEC female vs. male, Greek USHMM female vs. male, and Italian CDEC Italy vs. Rhodes.

Gender Analysis

The gender comparison page shows words ranked by frequency ratio (×N) — how many times more frequent a word is in women’s vs. men’s testimonies (or vice versa). Behind the scenes, words are pre-filtered using two additional tests that must agree on direction:

  • Burrows’ Zeta (Burrows 2007; Schöch 2018) — a document-level measure that checks in how many testimonies a word appears on each side, preventing a single long testimony from driving a result.
  • A statistical significance test to ensure the difference is not due to chance. Only confirmed words are shown.

A word only makes the list if it is both more frequent and more widespread across testimonies on one side.

Collocation Analysis

Collocations are word pairs that appear near each other far more often than chance would predict. On the site, pairs are ranked by Mutual Information (MI; Church & Hanks 1990), which measures how strongly two words are drawn to each other. High-MI pairs like camera + gas, stella + davide, or fame + sete (hunger + thirst) surface the distinctive vocabulary of testimony.

The table also shows raw co-occurrence frequency. Collocations are computed within a ±5 word window, with a minimum of 3 co-occurrences in at least 3 different testimonies.

Word Sketches & Multi-Word Expressions

Word sketches (Kilgarriff et al. 2004, 2014) extend collocation analysis by grouping co-occurrences according to grammatical relations extracted from dependency parses. For example, for a noun we can see what verbs take it as subject (what does this thing do?), what adjectives modify it (how is it described?), and what other nouns appear alongside it (what is it connected to?). Association strength is measured with logDice.

Multi-word expressions (MWEs) are extracted by two methods: dependency-based (compound and fixed relations in the parse tree) and PMI-filtered bigrams/trigrams (Church & Hanks 1990, PMI ≥ 5.0). This captures fixed phrases like campo di concentramento, croce rossa, carta d’identità.

Distributional Thesaurus

The distributional thesaurus ranks headwords by how similarly they are used in context, following the Sketch Engine approach (Kilgarriff et al. 2004; Rychlý & Kilgarriff 2007). Each headword is represented as a vector of (grammatical_relation, collocate) pairs weighted by logDice. Similarity is computed as cosine similarity between these vectors.

Two words are considered similar if they appear in the same kinds of sentences with the same kinds of neighboring words. Unlike neural embeddings, every similarity score can be traced back to specific shared collocates — you can see why the system considers two words related.

Cross-Lingual Comparison

Greek-Italian word pairs are curated from bilingual dictionaries and domain expertise. For each pair, word sketches from both languages are compared: shared collocates (words used similarly in both languages) are identified using cross-lingual embeddings, while unique collocates reveal language-specific associations.

For the pair πόλεμος (war, Greek) ↔ guerra (war, Italian), we can compare which words surround “war” in each language — revealing whether the two communities frame the same concept through similar or different vocabulary.

Gender Dimension

A central aim of this lexicon is to examine how female and male survivors narrate the same experiences through different vocabulary. The Italian CDEC corpus provides the cleanest gender comparison, with roughly balanced female (48) and male (53) testimonies from the same archive. The Greek USHMM comparison (13F vs 40M) is the most controlled Greek gender analysis, but the smaller female sample means results should be interpreted with caution.

Gender statistics combine document-level measures (checking how many testimonies use a word, not just how often) with frequency-based measures, to avoid inflating results from a few long testimonies.

Limitations

  • ASR transcription quality: Italian and Greek USHMM testimonies were transcribed automatically. Minimum frequency and range thresholds filter most artifacts, but some noise remains.
  • Greek lemmatization: Automatic lemmatization for Greek is less accurate than for Italian, especially for verb forms in oral speech. Some inflected forms may appear as separate entries rather than being grouped under one headword.
